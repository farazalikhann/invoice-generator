Photography Invoice Template

Photography work is rarely a single flat fee — it's a session rate, a retainer paid up front, hours of editing, and sometimes prints or a licensed digital gallery on top. An invoice that lists each of those separately does two things: it shows the client exactly what they're paying for, and it gives you a paper trail if a dispute ever comes up over what was included in a package.

The template below is pre-filled with a typical wedding-shoot breakdown — coverage, editing, and image delivery — so you can see the structure in action. Swap in your own package, rates, and client details, then download a PDF that's ready to send.

What to include on a photography invoice

  • Session date, time, and location
  • Package or coverage tier booked (hours, number of shooters)
  • Number of edited, delivered images
  • Print, product, or usage/licensing rights included
  • Travel fees or add-ons, itemized separately
  • Retainer already paid, and the balance still due

Photography invoice FAQ

When should I send a photography invoice?

Most photographers send a partial invoice (the retainer) at booking, then the final invoice within a few days of the shoot or once the edited gallery is delivered — whichever your contract specifies. Sending it promptly, while the client is still excited about the photos, tends to get you paid faster.

Should I require a deposit before shooting?

Yes. A retainer of 30-50% due at booking is standard in photography and protects your time against last-minute cancellations. List it as its own line item so the client can see exactly what's already been paid and what remains.

What if a client wants extra edited photos after delivery?

Treat it as a new billable line item on a fresh invoice — for example "Additional edited image, per photo." Setting that rate in your contract ahead of time makes it easy to invoice for extras without renegotiating.
